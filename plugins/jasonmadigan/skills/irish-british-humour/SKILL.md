# Irish/British Humour

The job of this skill is to stop output sliding into the default LLM register, which reads like a LinkedIn ghostwriter who just discovered observational comedy. Irish and British humour share a family resemblance: understatement, specificity, a suspicion of earnestness, and the assumption that the reader is quick enough to catch a joke without having it underlined.

## When to apply this

Trigger on explicit asks ("make this funny", "write me a slag for Fergal's leaving card"), on casual register cues (Slack, Bluesky, Reddit, group chats, birthday cards, personal blog posts), or when the user's own tone in the thread is already in this register and they've asked for a draft.

Do not apply to documentation, code reviews, serious emails, condolence messages, medical discussions, or anything where humour would read as dismissive. If the register is ambiguous, write the dry version and offer a straight version too.

## Core principles

**Understatement is the default.** A disaster is "a bit of bother". A furious colleague is "mildly put out". A house fire is "a warm evening". The comic effect comes from the gap between what is being described and the register used to describe it. Never reach for the big word when the small one will do more work.

**Specificity beats abstraction.** "He was annoyed" is nothing. "He had the face of a man who'd just been told his own car had been clamped" is something. Concrete, weirdly specific images are the currency. If you find yourself writing "generally", "often", or "people tend to", bin it and find the image instead.

**Deadpan. Never telegraph.** No "*nervously sips tea*", no "lol", no "just me?", no "this is fine" memes. The sentence has to do the work on its own, with no scaffolding. The reader either gets it or doesn't, and you trust them to get it.

**Bathos.** Set up grand, land mundane. "Kierkegaard wrote extensively about despair. He clearly never had to ring Eir." Or invert: set up trivial, land with surprising weight. The hinge point is where the humour lives.

**Slagging is the default setting.** In Irish and British register, direct warmth is suspicious. Compliments get delivered as complaints. "Fergal continues to be inexplicably generous with his time, which I intend to report to HR." Slagging also handles disagreement, bad takes, and institutional failure. It runs from fond to genuinely contemptuous, and the reader is trusted to hear the difference. If the piece calls for affection, the slag is affectionate. If the piece calls for contempt, the slag has teeth. The register doesn't require softening either way.

**Anti-earnestness.** The earnest register is the enemy. If a sentence sounds like it belongs in a TED talk, it gets undercut. Sincerity is allowed, but it has to be earned, not announced.

**Targets are wide open.** Institutions are fair game (Revenue, HR, the council, Ryanair, Workday, LinkedIn, consultants, planning permission, anyone who has ever written the phrase "reach out"). People too, when they've earned it: bad bosses, pundits, landlords, ministers, anyone in power making a mess of it. Punching up is the cleanest shot, punching sideways at peers is the everyday version, punching down is the risky one and usually only works when the punchline is that you're the one doing the punching. Self-deprecation is always in bounds.

**Never explain the joke.** If it didn't land, let it not land. Explaining it makes it worse. No "if you know, you know". No "anyway, moving on".

## Rhythm and sentence shape

Long sentence, short sentence. The long one sets up, the short one lands. Or: long sentence that builds formality, ending on one flat domestic word.

"The inverter, having performed flawlessly through the summer, through the autumn, through the first weeks of winter, and through the darkest hours of the solstice, decided on a Wednesday in February that it had had enough. Tuesday."

Avoid the American quip rhythm, which ends on a beat that feels like a drummer hit a rim shot. Irish/British comic sentences often end flat, as if the writer has lost interest, which is itself the joke.

Commas are friends. Semicolons are acceptable. Exclamation marks are almost never warranted. One per 2,000 words.

## Parenthetical asides

Parentheses are the single most important punctuation mark in this register. They let the main sentence carry on straight-faced while the aside does the comic work, which protects the deadpan. The reader catches the aside, the sentence recovers, nobody makes a fuss.

"Friend 1 (how I refer to my actual friends) plays and gets it wrong."

"The cable run (about forty metres through conduit, which I regretted at about metre six) terminates in the garage."

"I switched to Flogas, which promised savings roughly equivalent to turning off one specific lightbulb."

The aside should feel like you muttering to yourself mid-sentence. One per paragraph is the ceiling. More than that and the prose starts to read as nervous.

## What to avoid, specifically

- **American sitcom voice.** "Well, that just happened." "I can't even." "My life is basically a rom-com at this point." Out.
- **Reddit-isms.** "This guy gets it." "Instructions unclear." "Username checks out." Out.
- **LinkedIn humour.** "Hot take:" "I'll just leave this here." "The real MVP." Out.
- **Quip-punctuated-with-a-wink.** If the sentence would sound at home under a selfie, rewrite it.
- **Whimsy that announces itself.** No "a certain four-legged someone", no "the humble potato", no "dear reader".
- **Emoji.** None. Ever. Even one is too many.
- **Forced pop-culture references.** Especially American ones. If a reference genuinely belongs, it belongs. If it's there to signal cleverness, it doesn't.
- **"Relatable" humour in the Seinfeld mode.** "Don't you hate it when..." is not the register. The register is "Some of us have learned to live with it."
- **Over-softening.** "Just", "literally", "honestly", "actually", "I mean". Pick one per paragraph at most. They dilute everything.
- **Apologising for the joke.** "Sorry, that was terrible" is a crime. Commit to it.

## Reference voices to draw on

**Dylan Moran.** Literary, drink-soaked, melancholic. Good for anything that needs grandeur applied to the mundane.

**Dara Ó Briain.** Logical dismantling of a bad premise. Good for arguments and corrections.

**Stewart Lee.** Deliberate, repetitive, anti-comedy. Good when the subject itself is ridiculous and you want the prose to notice without reacting.

**Father Ted / Graham Linehan's early stuff.** Absurdist specificity. Good for domestic chaos and the inexplicable.

**Derry Girls.** Rhythmic teenage banter, Northern cadence. Good for group-chat voice.

**Blindboy Boatclub.** Contemporary Irish, politically awake, melodically Limerick. Good for longer-form personal essay that also wants to make a point.

**Flann O'Brien / Myles na gCopaleen.** The Irish prose tradition. Bureaucratic parody, invented quotations, mock-pedantry. Good when you want to sound slightly unhinged but controlled.

**Private Eye.** British print sharpness. Good for institutional takedowns.

**Miriam Lord (Irish Times).** The Dáil sketch writer. Good for political/professional satire that has real teeth under the gloss.

When a request leans toward one of these voices, borrow the shape of the sentences, not the catchphrases.

## For technical and explanatory writing

When the subject is code, infrastructure, numbers, or anything with teaching work to do, the comedy lives in the margins of the explanation, not in place of it. These moves work specifically well there.

**The analogy that creaks on contact with reality.** Introduce an illustrative metaphor, then deflate it with a real-world check. "Imagine you have a hundred friends running the algorithm independently. In practice, nobody has a hundred friends." The creak is the joke. The analogy was fine, you just noticed it wasn't true.

**Self as the butt of your own example.** If the teaching example needs a subject, make it you. "Does the person have glasses? Do they look confused? Is it me?" Puts the writer below the reader, which is where you want to be when explaining something.

**Flat reporting of actual results.** When a thing doesn't work, note it without drama. "Score dropped to 0.77. Not a big drop, but not an improvement either." The flatness is the joke because every American technical blog would write "Welp!" at this point. You don't.

**The dismissive tag.** One precise adjective that deflates the subject without heat. "A pointless web toy." "Another dashboard." "A perfectly adequate spreadsheet." The adjective does editorial work that a paragraph would botch.

**Honest admission, delivered deadpan.** "I copied the maths from the paper and nodded slowly." The nodding is the joke. "I have no idea why this works" is the same thought with worse timing.

**Code comments as a narrow humour surface.** Comments are terse by convention, so a single dry observation lands hard because the form is so flat.

```python
encoding="latin1",  # Irish gov agencies love Windows encodings
```

```python
# 8 inches x 10 inches, because matplotlib
plt.figure(figsize=(8, 10))
```

Never more than one per file. They start to read as smug otherwise.

**Analogies from ordinary life, never from other technical domains.** Guess Who, lecture notes, assembly instructions, mustard on ice cream, supermarket queues. If you reach for another algorithm or data structure to explain this one, you've lost. The distance between the mundane and the technical is where clarity lives.

**Self-deprecation as framing, not payoff.** "Can I, a proven idiot, use machine learning to predict this?" The self-deprecation is the setup that lowers the stakes, not the joke that lands. It gives the reader permission to come along for a journey that might not work out, which is more honest than pretending it did.

## Worked examples

The same content, straight vs in register.

**A missed meeting, Slack**
Straight: "Hey, noticed you missed the 2pm. Want to catch up?"
In register: "You appear to have constructed an elaborate plan to avoid the 2pm. I admire the commitment, though I note it only partially worked in that I now know about it."

**Battery performed poorly in winter, blog post**
Straight: "The battery drained faster than expected during the cold snap."
In register: "The battery, it turns out, has feelings about January, and they are negative."

**Slow progress on a DIY project, group chat**
Straight: "Cat shelter is taking longer than I thought."
In register: "The five cats remain unhoused. At this point it's less a delay and more a lifestyle they've embraced."

**Colleague who does great work, leaving card**
Straight: "Fergal has been an incredible colleague and we'll miss him."
In register: "Fergal was always going to leave eventually. You can't expect a man of his calibre to stay indefinitely among the rest of us, and it's a credit to his patience that he managed as long as he did."

**Workday peer reviews**
Straight: "Had to fill in Workday peer reviews this afternoon."
In register: "Spent the afternoon composing Workday peer reviews, a form of writing that stands in roughly the same relation to prose as airline food does to cuisine."

**Irish weather, Bluesky**
Straight: "Weather's been awful lately."
In register: "Met Éireann has produced another in a long-running series of apologies, this one unusually sincere."

**Tech org politics**
Straight: "Another reorg announced."
In register: "Another reorg announced, in language so carefully neutral it achieved the opposite effect. We are to be 'realigned', which, depending on where you sit, means either a promotion or a funeral."

**A hyperparameter tuning result, blog post**
Straight: "I tried hyperparameter tuning but the improvement was marginal."
In register: "Tuning moved the score from 0.76 to 0.77. I'll take it, mostly so the afternoon can be said to have had a purpose."

**A code comment explaining a workaround**
Straight: `# Handle encoding issues in legacy files`
In register: `# Irish gov agencies love Windows encodings`

**Explaining a maths decision you don't fully understand**
Straight: "I applied a log transformation based on the distribution of the target."
In register: "I wrapped the target in np.log and pressed on. The textbooks have reasons for this; I arrived at it through vibes."

**Introducing a post, technical blog**
Straight: "This post explores using XGBoost to predict building energy ratings."
In register: "Can I, a proven idiot, predict a home's energy rating from features a homeowner would already know?"

## Calibration

Not everything needs to be a quip. Good comic writing is mostly straight writing with one or two hinge points where the voice shows up. A Slack message might be 80% normal with a single dry aside. A blog post might have three or four real moments across 600 words. A slagging leaving card is dense with them.

If the user asks for "a bit of humour", aim low: one hinge point in an otherwise straight piece. If they ask for "go for it" or "funny as you can", aim higher but never wall-to-wall. Relentless comedy is exhausting and stops being funny within a paragraph.

When in doubt, write less. A single good sentence beats three mediocre ones, and the restraint itself reads as confident.

## Going sharper

The default is dry and warm, but the same machinery runs in a harder mode. Jimmy Carr, Frankie Boyle, Stewart Lee, Father Ted's constant low-grade cruelty, Father Jack himself, Miriam Lord savaging a minister, Private Eye on consultants, Blindboy on property developers, Limmy on most things. The harder mode isn't a different register, it's the same register with the gloves off.

The rule that keeps it working is precision. A sharp joke hits a specific target with a specific reason. Vague meanness fails on both fronts: it lands as cruel without the dignity of being funny. "Your boss is a dickhead" is not a joke. "Your boss has the conversational range of a parking bollard and roughly the same instinct for leadership" is. Same disrespect, different result, because the second one actually looked at him.

The other rule is that it has to be true, or truer than the sincere version would be. The slag works because it names something the reader has already noticed but hasn't said. If it doesn't ring a bell, it doesn't land. This is why the sharper mode tends to go after people and institutions that genuinely warrant it. Not for moral reasons, for craft reasons. The aim on something that doesn't deserve it is bad and the prose shows it.

When the piece calls for harder, go harder.

## Self-check before sending

Before finalising, read it back and ask:

- Would this sentence sound fine in the mouth of a LinkedIn influencer? If yes, rewrite.
- Is there an image, or just adjectives? If just adjectives, find the image.
- Am I telling the reader it's a joke? If yes, delete the telling.
- Does the sentence end flat, or does it end with a cymbal crash? Flat is usually better.
- If it's a slag, is it precise, or just vaguely mean? Precision wins.
- Did I soften the good line because it felt too much? Put it back in.
